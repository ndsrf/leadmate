# If Your Dog Bites Other People

## heading
Biting someone other than you

## hook
A guest extends a hand, a child runs toward you, or a stranger tries to pet them on the street. {name} growls, lunges, or snaps. You are left shaken, embarrassed, and terrified it might happen again. It is not your fault, but preventing a recurrence is in your hands, and that starts with protecting everyone involved.

## why
Almost always, it stems from fear. A Dog who feels cornered or invaded first gives warnings: moving away, yawning, lip licking, side-eyeing, stiffening, growling. If those warnings are ignored or punished, biting is all that remains. Biting can also stem from pain, resource guarding, or overwhelming arousal. The more it repeats, the more they learn that biting succeeds in making people back away.

## steps
1. Protect people right now: use a leash outside, set up a baby gate or closed door at home when guests visit, and never leave them alone with children.
2. Ask visitors to ignore them: no direct eye contact, talking, or reaching out. Let {name} choose whether to approach.
3. Learn their warning signs: yawning, lip licking, side-eye, a stiff body, growling. The moment you spot them, increase distance immediately.
4. Avoid overwhelming situations: crowded sidewalks, strangers reaching out to pet, children running up. When in doubt, add distance.
5. When a person appears at a distance where {name} can still eat calmly, deliver a treat. This pairs people with positive experiences, without exceeding their comfort zone.
6. Seek guidance from a veterinary behaviorist before exposing them to more people. A tailored plan is far safer than experimenting on your own.

## example
### {name} and the eight o'clock guest
A friend arrives for dinner. Before opening the door, you guide {name} to another room with a stuffed food toy and close the door. Your friend enters without looking at the Dog. Later, with {name} several meters away on a leash, your friend tosses a treat onto the floor without approaching. {name} eats it calmly and looks at you. That is enough for today: the visit ends before fear ever surfaces, and that is a real success.

## compare
### do
Give {name} space as soon as warning signs appear, and introduce people gradually with guidance from a veterinary behaviorist if a bite has occurred.
### dont
Force greetings or allow a stranger to pet them to "get them used to it": that increases fear and elevates bite risk.

## tip
Today: write down the three situations involving people that are hardest for {name}, and plan how to avoid each one this week. To practice calm with hands nearby, play "Calm Hands and Leash" in World 1: it is calm practice, not bite training.

## takeaways
- Safety for everyone comes first.
- Biting almost always stems from fear, not malice.
- Respect warning signs and increase distance immediately.
- A veterinary behaviorist guides you safely.
