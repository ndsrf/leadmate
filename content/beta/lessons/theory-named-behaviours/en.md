# It Has a Name

## heading
"My Dog is just like that"

## hook
They lie flat on the sidewalk watching an approaching dog and, once close, suddenly lunge. At night, they sprint in frantic circles down the hallway. Nonstop barking at the window. Sometimes, when held back, they mouth your jacket sleeve. It is easy to assume that something is uniquely strange about {name}, or that this is just their personality and nothing can be done. But almost everything they do has a name.

## why
Dogs share a common behavioral language. What your Dog does is performed by thousands of other dogs for the exact same reasons: excitement, fear, habit, or pent-up energy. Canine professionals have observed these patterns so often that they have given them names. That is empowering news: if a behavior has a name, it has a recognized cause and an established first step that works. It is not your fault, nor your Dog's, and it is not an unchangeable personality trait. It is a state or learned pattern, and it can be trained.

## steps
1. The turtle: lying down and waiting, locked onto an oncoming dog, usually reacting once close. Lying down here is a warning sign, not calm. Why: high tension anticipating the encounter. First step: do not wait to see what happens; create distance or keep moving before the other dog arrives.
2. Zoomies: full-speed circular sprinting, often at the end of the day or after a bath, especially in puppies. Why: sudden discharge of pent-up energy and arousal. First step: do not chase or hype them up; provide space, wait it out, and ensure more sniffing and walk time during the day.
3. Reacting to other dogs: barking, pulling, or lunging at other dogs on leash. Why: usually fear, barrier frustration from being unable to greet, or intense arousal. First step: increase distance; you will train this in the street Worlds.
4. Redirected biting: grabbing your sleeve or pant leg when overaroused or blocked from reaching a goal. Why: tension must find an outlet and you are nearby. First step: do not scold; calmly move away from what excites them without abrupt movements and wait for calm.
5. Nonstop barking: barking incessantly at the window, a sound, or at you. Why: arousal is so high that processing information is blocked. First step: do not shout or give Cues; wait for calm to return. You will explore this in "Calm First".

## example
### {name} and the doorway dog
You walk along your sidewalk and, in the distance, a dog emerges from an entryway. {name} drops flat to the pavement, eyes locked and body rigid. You used to assume they were tired. Now you recognize the turtle. You do not wait to see what happens: you say their name once, turn around, and cross to the opposite sidewalk. {name} gets up and follows. From a distance, the other dog passes without incident. Next time you turn a little earlier, and {name} does not even drop to the ground.

## compare
### do
Name what you observe and apply its first step: the turtle asks for distance, zoomies ask for space, and nonstop barking asks for calm.
### dont
Assume your Dog is just wired that way with no solution, or take it personally as if done out of spite.

## tip
Try it today: write down one behavior your Dog does that you thought was unique to them. Find its name in this lesson and test its first step.

## takeaways
- What your Dog does is shared by thousands of dogs.
- If it has a name, it has a cause and a first step.
- It is not a flaw: it is canine behavior, and it can be guided.
