# Reading Body Language

## heading
It feels like barking out of nowhere

## hook
You are walking on the sidewalk and, suddenly, {name} barks at a dog passing across the street. It seems to come out of nowhere: a second ago they were sniffing along as usual. But it almost never happens out of the blue. Before barking or lunging, your Dog was giving you silent warnings with their body. You just need to know where to look.

## why
Your Dog does not tell you how they feel with words, but with their ears, tail, mouth, and body weight. When something worries or excites them, their body shifts gradually: the mouth closes, they freeze, and weight shifts forward. If nothing around them changes, emotional tension keeps rising until barking or pulling is all that is left. They are not doing this to defy you: they are overwhelmed, and an overwhelmed Dog cannot learn. Reading your Dog in time gives you the most valuable asset on a walk: a few seconds to help before anything happens.

## steps
1. At home, while resting, observe their ears, tail, mouth, and posture. This is your Dog at baseline calm: your starting reference.
2. On the walk, check in frequently and always in the same sequence: ears, tail, body weight, and mouth. Two seconds is all it takes.
3. Look for the shifts: the mouth closes, they freeze, weight shifts to the front paws, and the tail rises stiff.
4. Also notice calming signals, subtle gestures asking for space: yawning when not tired, lip licking, turning the head away, or shaking off.
5. As soon as you spot the first signal, gain distance: turn around calmly, cross the street, or step back. When in doubt, add more distance.
6. When they return to sniffing or look back at you with a relaxed mouth, mark that instant with a soft Marker and deliver a treat.

## example
### {name} and the park dog
You enter the park and, in the distance, a grey dog appears. {name} stops dead in their tracks: ears prick forward, the mouth closes, and the tail rises, rigid. Not a single bark yet. You turn around calmly, give a gentle "let's go" Cue, and walk twenty paces away toward the bench. At first, it is hard for them to tear their eyes away from the other dog. Shortly after, the tail lowers and they begin sniffing the grass: you use your Marker and deliver a treat. From there, you watch the grey dog pass without a single bark. Another day, you might be able to stand a little closer.

## compare
### do
Look at the entire body, not just the tail. If the tail wags but the mouth is shut and the body is stiff, gain distance.
### dont
Assume that because the tail is wagging, they are happy, and keep approaching. A high, stiff, fast-wagging tail usually signals tension, not joy.

## tip
Try it today: before heading out, watch your Dog for one minute while resting. Note their ears, tail, and mouth: that is what calm looks like.

## takeaways
- Before barking or pulling, their body warns you.
- Always check in sequence: ears, tail, weight, and mouth.
- A wagging tail does not always mean happiness.
- At the first signal, create distance; once calm returns, reward.
