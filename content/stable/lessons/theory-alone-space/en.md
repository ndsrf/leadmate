# Being Left Alone and the Safe Space

## heading
Whining, barking, or destroying things when you leave

## hook
You pick up your keys and {name} is already shadowing you down the hallway. You leave for ten minutes, and upon returning, you hear crying from the stairwell or find a shredded cushion by the door. If barking or destruction happens only while you are away, this is what this topic covers: learning to stay alone without distress.

## why
Staying alone is not something dogs know how to do automatically: it is learned, step by step. A familiar, cozy space, such as an exercise pen with an open crate, provides clear boundaries and supports calm—always as a haven, never as punishment. If a step is too big, the Dog does not adapt: fear takes over. That is why progress happens in steps small enough to handle comfortably, starting with the door wide open, without pressure or abrupt confinement.

## steps
1. Set up {name}'s resting space—the one you prepared in the rest-at-home lesson—and add an exercise pen with an open crate. Never use it as a punishment.
2. With the door open, let them enter voluntarily for a treat and leave whenever they wish. Repeat until they enter calmly; do not close anything yet.
3. When they lie down inside, step a few paces away and return before restlessness starts. Gradually lengthen the duration each day, without pressure or calling them.
4. Practice the micro-close: close the gate for one second, open it, and give your release Cue—the phrase that means "you are free to come out." It is never a demand.
5. Before leaving, go for a walk and keep departures low-key, without fanfare. Upon returning, greet calmly without fuss until settled.
6. If whining occurs, distinguish protest from panic. With puppies under 4 months, wait at most 2 minutes; with dogs 4 months or older, up to 10 minutes of continuous crying. If panic arises, or time expires, open the door.

## example
### {name} and the pen gate
It is Saturday and {name} has been practicing for three days. Today they enter the pen, lie down, and chew their bone. You take two steps back, return, and speak the release Cue: "all done". Next, you close the gate for a second; {name} whimpers once, but breathes normally and resumes chewing. You wait, open the gate, and celebrate calmly. Later, during another attempt, drooling and scratching at the gate occur: you open immediately, and tomorrow return to a smaller step. This is not a failure: it is how pace is calibrated.

## compare
### do
Open the door as soon as panic appears or time is up, and return to an easier step the next day.
### dont
Let them cry "until they tire themselves out" without a time limit. If panic sets in, crying is not learning: it is fear.

## tip
Try it today: with the door open, leave a treat inside the resting area and see if {name} enters voluntarily and lingers for a moment.

## takeaways
- Being alone is learned in small, unforced steps.
- A dedicated space is a haven, never a punishment.
- With protest, wait; with panic or time up, open the door.
- Puppies under 4 months wait far less than adults.
