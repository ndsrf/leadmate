# Choosing the Cue

## heading
Food, scooters, another dog: what do you say?

## hook
You walk along a bustling street. {name} spots a piece of sandwich on the sidewalk and, almost simultaneously, a scooter rounds the corner. You already know "Leave It" and "Let's Go", but in that second your mind goes blank. You blurt out one, then the other, and before you can react {name} is already pulling. You do not lack Cues: what is missing is knowing which one fits each moment.

## why
Each Cue solves a different problem. Something stationary on the ground is not going anywhere: {name} can disengage, look at you, and collect a reward from your hand. That is why "Leave It" works for food on the ground. Moving Distractors, by contrast, keep approaching, and every second ramps up arousal or fear. Asking your Dog to ignore something as it bears down on them is asking too much. Moving away together with "Let's Go" provides distance, and distance is what lowers arousal. If you mix or repeat Cues, neither delivers a clear signal. A single, well-timed Cue does.

## steps
1. Scan the street ahead, not just your Dog. Spot potential Distractors early: dropped food, wheels, other dogs.
2. Ask yourself one simple question: is it stationary or moving? Stationary on the ground, "Leave It". Moving or approaching, "Let's Go".
3. Speak the Cue once, in a calm voice, before they reach the object or become fixated.
4. Reward promptly: with "Leave It", deliver a treat from your hand when they look at you; with "Let's Go", reward as soon as they turn and follow you.
5. If neither applies or they fail to respond, ask for nothing more: create distance. Cross the street, take an alternate route, or head home.

## example
### {name} and the market street
It is Saturday and you are strolling down the market street. {name} raises a nose: a chunk of bread lies beside a bench. It is stationary, so two meters away you say "Leave It" once. {name} looks at it, hesitates, and checks in with you: a treat from your hand. A little further along, a large dog exits a doorway heading your way. {name} tenses up, ears pricked forward. That is moving: "Let's Go"—you turn and walk in the opposite direction. The first time takes a second to follow; when they do, a reward. Ten meters later they are walking beside you on a Slack leash.

## compare
### do
Decide early and speak a single Cue. If there is no response, distance does the rest: move away together and cue again from further away.
### dont
Wait to see what happens until the Distractor is right on top of you, then repeat the Cue louder and louder. Proximity erases listening ability.

## tip
Try it today: during your walk, whisper what you spot before your Dog notices: "on the ground" or "moving". Just that, without cueing anything.

## takeaways
- Stationary on the ground, "Leave It"; moving, "Let's Go".
- One timely Cue is worth more than two late ones.
- If neither fits or there is no response, add distance.
